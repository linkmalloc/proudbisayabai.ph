In pageantry, there is no universal recipe for success. What satisfies one panel of judges may not necessarily appeal to another.

  

Is pageantry becoming a cooking show?

  

It may sound like an unusual comparison, but there is an interesting parallel between the two. In a cooking competition, contestants prepare their dishes using different ingredients, techniques, and presentations. Each hopes to impress the panel of judges. Yet, at the end of the day, the winning dish is determined not only by how it is prepared but also by how it is evaluated.

  

The same dynamic can be observed in pageantry.

  

Every candidate enters a competition with a distinct personality, preparation, advocacy, communication skills, stage presence, and overall presentation. Each brings a personal interpretation of what it means to be a titleholder. However, the final verdict rests on a panel of judges whose individual perspectives, expectations, and preferences may vary.

  

This brings us to one of the realities of pageantry: winning one competition does not necessarily guarantee winning another.

  

A candidate who previously earned a crown may join another pageant and discover that the qualities that made them successful before are not necessarily the same qualities valued by a different panel of judges.

  

One panel may appreciate elegance, confidence, and sophistication. Another may prioritize intelligence, authenticity, relatability, advocacy, or the ability to communicate effectively. Neither approach is automatically wrong, provided the evaluation follows the established criteria of the competition.

  

Does that make a candidate less deserving when they do not win? Not necessarily.

  

Just like food, taste is subjective. Some judges appreciate bold and unconventional flavors, while others prefer something classic and familiar. A dish can be carefully prepared, beautifully presented, and technically excellent, yet it may not become everyone’s favorite.

  

The same can happen in pageantry.

  

But this raises a bigger question: Are pageant results a reflection of a candidate’s overall performance, or are they sometimes influenced by the personal preferences of those sitting at the judges’ table?

  

This is where the responsibility of the judging panel becomes important.

  

Judges are not expected to have identical opinions. Their diverse backgrounds and perspectives can enrich the evaluation process. However, personal taste should never completely overshadow the official judging criteria. Consistency, transparency, fairness, and objectivity must remain at the center of every competition.

  

For pageant organizers, establishing clear criteria and ensuring that judges understand the standards of evaluation are essential. A crown carries responsibility, and the process of awarding it should command the respect and confidence of both candidates and audiences.

  

For candidates, the lesson is equally meaningful: do not allow one competition’s result to define your entire pageant journey.

  

A previous victory is an achievement, but it is not a lifetime guarantee of winning. Likewise, losing another competition does not erase your accomplishments, preparation, or potential. Every stage presents a different opportunity to learn, improve, and discover new strengths.

  

Perhaps this is the beauty and frustration of pageantry. You can prepare the finest ingredients, perfect your recipe, and deliver your best presentation, but the outcome may still depend on the people evaluating your work.

  

A crown may be awarded by the judges, but a candidate’s worth should never be measured by their personal preferences.

  

At the end of the day, pageantry should be more than a matter of taste. It should be a celebration of excellence, character, intelligence, purpose, and representation.

  

Because just like a cooking competition, not everyone will have the same favorite dish. But every contestant deserves a fair evaluation based on the standards of the competition.

  

And perhaps the real recipe for a credible pageant is not simply finding a winner, but ensuring that the process of choosing one is worthy of the crown.
