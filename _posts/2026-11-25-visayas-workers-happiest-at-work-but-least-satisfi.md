Workers in the Visayas reported the country's highest workplace happiness but were also the least likely to consider themselves fairly paid, highlighting a gap between job satisfaction and compensation, a study by Jobstreet by SEEK showed.

  

The region posted an 82% workplace happiness rating, exceeding Mindanao's 79%, Metro Manila's 77% and Luzon's 76%, according to the Workplace Happiness Index: Philippines 2025.

  

However, only 74% of Visayas employees considered themselves fairly or well paid, the lowest regional figure and below the national average of 80%, based on the Salary Pulse: Philippines 2026 report.

  

Jobstreet by SEEK presented the findings during a study dissemination on Wednesday, November 25, in Lapu-Lapu City, examining salary perceptions, workplace happiness and hiring trends across regions, industries and generations.

  

The 2026 salary survey covered 1,009 employed Filipinos aged 18 to 64 in February, while the workplace happiness study surveyed roughly 1,000 workers nationwide.

  

Nationally, 77% of workers reported being happy at work. Although 80% considered their pay fair or good, only 59% said they were genuinely happy with their salaries. Another 45% said their earnings were insufficient to cover their daily living expenses.

  

Mindanao recorded the highest share of workers who considered themselves fairly or well paid at 87%, but 54% still said their salaries fell short of the cost of living.

  

The findings also pointed to differences in living costs between urban centers and provincial areas as a factor shaping workers' perceptions of their earnings.

  

Joey Yusingco, Jobstreet by SEEK head of country marketing, said employers should distinguish between fair compensation and actual salary satisfaction.

  

“Fair pay is not the same as salary happiness,” Yusingco said during his presentation.

  

He said salary satisfaction affects employee motivation and their likelihood of remaining with their employers.

  

Yusingco also discussed differences in salary ranges across industries and job levels, based on vacancies posted on the platform. Information and communications technology and other technology-related positions generally offered higher salary ranges because of strong demand and limited availability of skilled workers.

  

He noted that many technology job advertisements now require applicants to have skills in artificial intelligence and generative AI tools, which can accelerate coding and other technology-related tasks.

  

Frontline and support positions generally offered more modest salary ranges than management roles. Yusingco also cited the growing impact of AI on repetitive and entry-level jobs, including some positions in the business process outsourcing industry.

  

He cautioned that the figures represented salary ranges in job advertisements and did not necessarily reflect the compensation of every worker or position in the market.

  

Employers, he said, should treat market salary data as a reference rather than the sole basis for setting compensation, since expectations differ across generations, industries and locations.

  

The study also linked salary satisfaction to employee behavior. Workers satisfied with their pay were 2.2 times more likely to go beyond their assigned duties, while those dissatisfied with their salaries were 2.9 times more likely to consider changing jobs.

  

When salary increases fail to meet expectations, employees may negotiate with their employers, seek additional benefits, remain in their positions with reduced motivation or look for other jobs.

  

The survey found that 28% of workers would consider relocating for a 10% salary increase. Only a few, however, said they would accept the same increase in exchange for working in a toxic culture or under a disrespectful supervisor.

  

Yusingco urged employers to make compensation discussions more transparent and give workers regular opportunities to raise concerns about their pay.

  

He encouraged companies to conduct check-ins through immediate supervisors or human resources personnel instead of waiting for annual performance reviews. Employers could also establish structured salary reviews that consider prevailing market rates and economic conditions.

  

The report also cited flexible work arrangements, career advancement and professional development as non-monetary benefits that could help improve employee satisfaction and retention.
