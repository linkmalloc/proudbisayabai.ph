The Supreme Court has dismissed petitions challenging the arrest and transfer of former president Rodrigo Duterte to the International Criminal Court (ICC), ruling that Philippine courts can no longer enforce a writ of habeas corpus because he is now detained in The Hague, Netherlands.

In a decision written by Associate Justice Japar B. Dimaampao, the SC En Banc denied the consolidated petitions filed by Duterte’s children Sebastian, Veronica and Paolo on behalf of their father.

Nine justices voted to dismiss the petitions, while four dissented. Associate Justice Samuel H. Gaerlan was absent on official business.

The petitions questioned Duterte’s arrest on March 11, 2025, when authorities apprehended him upon his arrival in the Philippines from Hong Kong based on an ICC warrant accusing him of crimes against humanity under the Rome Statute.

Duterte filed a petition for certiorari and prohibition before the SC later that day, seeking to stop Philippine government officials from cooperating with the ICC. He was flown to The Hague that evening.

His children filed separate petitions for habeas corpus the following day, arguing that the ICC warrant could not be enforced in the Philippines because the country had withdrawn from the Rome Statute. They also claimed that the arrest violated the Constitution, Philippine laws and international law.

The Office of the Solicitor General argued that the petitions had become moot because Philippine courts could not enforce a writ of habeas corpus outside the country. It maintained that the arrest complied with the Philippines' international obligations.

The SC agreed, ruling that habeas corpus is a domestic remedy that can only be enforced within Philippine territory.

Since Duterte is already under ICC custody in The Hague, Philippine courts cannot issue an enforceable order affecting his detention or release, the Court said.

“Significantly, \[Duterte\] is not just beyond the Court’s coercive powers, but also stands accused before an international tribunal that has assumed jurisdiction over his person,” the SC said.

The Court also noted that Duterte is participating in ICC proceedings and has pursued remedies available under the international tribunal's rules. His children were likewise aware of the grounds for his detention and were challenging it before the ICC.

It said issuing a writ would serve no practical purpose because habeas corpus examines the legality of detention before a person is transferred to a court.

The SC further ruled that the Philippine officials named in the petitions could no longer be compelled to produce or release Duterte because his arrest and transfer had already been completed.

The officials no longer had custody or control over him, regardless of whether the arrest and transfer were lawful, the Court said.

However, the dismissal does not establish that Duterte’s arrest and transfer were lawful or that his constitutional rights were not violated.

The SC clarified that its decision only addressed whether habeas corpus remained an available and enforceable remedy under the circumstances.

Any alleged violation of Duterte’s rights, including claims for compensation or liability, must be pursued through the appropriate legal proceedings instead of a habeas corpus petition, the Court said.

It also clarified that it was not ruling on whether Duterte could properly seek relief before the ICC, saying related issues were more appropriately addressed in a separate pending case, G.R. No. 278747.

In a concurring and dissenting opinion, Senior Associate Justice Marvic M.V.F. Leonen agreed that the petitions should be dismissed and joined the majority in holding that no legal barriers prevent Duterte’s prosecution before the ICC.

However, Leonen disagreed with the majority's failure to address what he described as unresolved and serious constitutional questions that could recur but evade judicial review.

He said the Court missed an opportunity to provide guidance to judges, lawyers and law enforcement agencies on the application of constitutional provisions, Republic Act No. 9851, or the Philippine Act on Crimes Against International Humanitarian Law, Genocide and Other Crimes Against Humanity, and the country's obligations under the Rome Statute.
